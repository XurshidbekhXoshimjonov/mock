SECTION 3 Tutor: Today we're discussing marine conservation, specifically focusing on New Zealand's dolphins. Fran, you've been researching this - could you start by telling us about how dolphins communicate? Fran: Yes, well, dolphins make sounds in a fascinating way. They don't have vocal cords like humans. Instead, they have nasal sacs in their heads. But what's really interesting is recent research showing that these sounds might have specific meanings - like names for each other. Tutor: That's remarkable. Now, about Hector's dolphins - what are the main threats they face? Fran: Hector's dolphins are particularly vulnerable to fishing nets. They get caught in them and drown. Their slow breeding rate doesn't help - females only have one calf every 2-3 years. Interestingly, tourist numbers don't seem to affect them much as they tend to avoid busy areas. Tutor: And what about conservation efforts? Fran: The New Zealand Government set up a marine sanctuary on the west coast. This wasn't because of public pressure or scientific research, but specifically to protect a major dolphin feeding ground in that area. The largest population of Hector's dolphins is actually found around the east coast of the South Island. Tutor: Let's talk about different dolphin species around New Zealand. What are their characteristics? Fran: Well, common dolphins are often seen swimming along behind boats. Dusky dolphins, on the other hand, swim in unusually large groups - sometimes hundreds together. Hector's dolphins tend to be more solitary. Maui's dolphins are critically endangered and live only off New Zealand's northwest coast. Tutor: What about orcas and bottle-nose dolphins? Fran: Orcas, while actually the largest dolphin species, are often mistaken for whales. In New Zealand, they're the second largest dolphin species. Bottle-nose dolphins are seen mostly in Kaikoura, where they've become quite a tourist attraction.
SECTION 4 Lecturer: Today we're looking at the Lontar palm, a remarkable tree from the Indonesian island of Rote. Known as the 'tree of life' by locals, it provides food, shelter, and materials for the community. The tree produces a sweet juice that's collected by climbers. The fruit resembles a coconut, but only female trees bear fruit. Climbers fix stones to the tree trunks to help them get a better grip. They keep their tools attached to a belt around their waist. The juice quickly becomes sour if left unprocessed, so it needs to be treated immediately. A concentrated form is drunk during the rainy season, and it can also be made into sugary biscuits. Other parts of the tree are equally useful. The leaves are woven into containers, bags and roofing materials. They're also used as garden fertiliser. During celebrations, brightly decorated hats made from the leaves are worn. The leaves can even be made into a musical instrument that sounds like a harp. The stalk is strong and flexible, ideal for making roads in the village. The trunk, being durable and termite-resistant, is used in the construction of houses. This one tree demonstrates how traditional communities make sustainable use of their natural resources, with virtually every part of the Lontar palm serving a practical purpose.
